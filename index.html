The problem

Insiders use valid credentials, so no rule breaks. Their behavior still drifts from their own normal.

What a behavioral baseline checks

Verdict: unusual for this person. Risk raised.
Goal

Predict insider threats by monitoring login and session behavior and flagging deviations from each user's own history, not from a company-wide average.

Technique

Behavioral analysis with anomaly detection tools. An unsupervised Isolation Forest scores each session, so no labeled attacks are needed for training.

More insiders found

At similar noise levels (6.1% against 4.6% false alarms) the Isolation Forest catches 44% of malicious sessions. The fixed rule catches 17%.

AUC tells a different story

The z-score rule ranks sessions slightly better overall (0.934 against 0.898), but its fixed cutoff throws most of that advantage away.

Still modest

Precision is 31%, so about two in three alerts are false. Insiders also behave normally for part of the test period, which caps per-session scores.

Accepted formats

1. Logon events (CERT style). Needs a user column, a timestamp column and an activity column. Each Logon is paired with its Logoff to form a session.

id,date,user,pc,activity
1,2025-01-01 08:55:00,EMP0001,PC-1010,Logon
2,2025-01-01 17:10:00,EMP0001,PC-1010,Logoff

2. Daily feature table. One row per user per day with numeric columns, such as login_hour, session_duration_mins, usb_events_count. A label column (is_malicious, label, insider) is used to measure accuracy if present.

Answer key (optional). A second CSV of known insiders: user,date pairs, CERT user,start,end windows, or a plain list of users.

Users with too little history (under 8 sessions or 6 days) are skipped, because there is no baseline to compare against.

Case files

Peak scores alone do not separate insiders, because 76 of 126 users touch 70 at least once. Sustained risk, the average over all of a user's sessions, does: four insiders sit between 54 and 68 while the highest employee is 38.

Presenter notes

A two-minute walkthrough in speaking order, the numbers worth remembering, and questions a reviewer is likely to ask.

The problem

Insiders already have valid access, so access rules cannot catch them. We need to notice when someone stops behaving like themselves.

15 sec

The idea

We treat login and session behavior as a behavioral biometric: when you log in, how long you stay, which machine you use and how often you reconnect.

20 sec

The data

We generated 90 days of logs for 126 users in the same format as the CMU CERT logon file. Six of them are insiders whose behavior turns anomalous partway through. The real CERT files plug into the same pipeline.

20 sec

The method

The first half of each user's history is their baseline. Every later session becomes five deviation features. An Isolation Forest scores how easy that session is to isolate, and we rescale the score to 0 to 100.

30 sec

The result

On 4,117 unseen sessions the Isolation Forest reaches F1 0.367 against 0.177 for a z-score rule and catches 44% of malicious sessions. By sustained risk, four of six insiders stand clear of every employee.

25 sec

Limits and next steps

Two insiders who only switched machines blend in, and about 6% of normal sessions still raise a false alarm. Next: the real CERT data, sequence models, more biometric signals and explanations for analysts.

Questions you may get

Why login and session behavior?

It needs no special hardware, every organization already logs it, and insiders use valid credentials, so the only thing that changes is behavior. Keystroke and mouse dynamics are natural extensions.

Why an Isolation Forest?

Attacks are rare and unlabeled, so a supervised classifier has nothing to learn from. An Isolation Forest is unsupervised: rare, unusual points take few random splits to isolate, so they get high anomaly scores. It is fast and easy to explain.

Why compare each user with their own baseline?

Normal differs by person and role. A 3 AM login is routine for one engineer and alarming for another. Z-scores against a person's own history capture that, and a company-wide average would not.

Is the data real?

Both. The headline results use synthetic logs in the CERT logon.csv schema, with insiders planted at known dates, so ground truth is exact. The Your data section also loads real CMU CERT r4.2 samples (published on GitHub) and accepts any CSV you upload, including the full CERT logon.csv. Everything is analyzed in your browser.

How does it do on the real CERT data?

Honestly, about the same as the simple rule. On the real labeled sample the Isolation Forest scores AUC 0.857 and F1 0.295, against 0.845 and 0.308 for the z-score rule. Real insiders are subtler than the planted attacks, so the large advantage on synthetic data is partly because those attacks are crisp. Richer signals (USB, files, email) are the next step.

Why is the z-score rule's AUC higher, but its F1 lower?

AUC measures ranking across every possible threshold. The rule ranks well but its alert cutoff is fixed at the 97th percentile of normal behavior, which catches only 17% of malicious sessions. F1 is measured at the actual cutoff, where the Isolation Forest does better. A tuned cutoff would narrow the gap, and it is fair to say so.

What are the weaknesses?

Precision is 31%, and the two insiders who only used unfamiliar machines have sustained risk of 29 and 32, inside the normal range. Rare binary signals like a new device carry little weight in an Isolation Forest. Per-day aggregation, a dedicated device rule and richer features are the fixes.

Is monitoring employees ethical?

The system uses metadata only: times, durations and machines, not content. A score is a prompt for human review, never an automatic penalty. A real deployment also needs a clear policy, employee notice and legal review.

What would you do next?

Run on CERT r4.2 with its answer key, add an LSTM autoencoder for sequences, fuse more signals such as file, email and keystroke data, update baselines as behavior drifts, and add SHAP-style explanations to each alert.
